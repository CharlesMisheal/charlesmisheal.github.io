1. Integrating Faith and Work

My work in technology is not separate from my discipleship. I want every project to begin with prayer and the question, "Who is this meant to bless?" Elder Bednar teaches that the Lord gathers "all things in Christ," so my studies, career, and faith can strengthen each other like intertwined strands. When I built Bravick, my offline-first school platform, I saw it as more than code. It was a way to serve schools that need reliable tools. As I move into AI evaluation, this principle reminds me that honesty and integrity are part of the work, not extras.

2. Ministering to the One

Behind every system is a real person: a student, a teacher, a family. Christ ministered to people one by one, and I want my technology to do the same. That means building tools that meet people where they are, such as supporting several languages and working without internet, protecting their data, and making sure AI gives them truthful answers. Teaching young people Python, data science, and machine learning at CloudCity is one of the ways I live this principle.

3. Consistency in Small and Simple Things

"By small and simple things are great things brought to pass" (Alma 37:6). Spiritual strength and professional skill both grow through daily habits. For me, that means morning scripture study before I open my laptop, steady progress in my cloud computing courses, and regular entries in this portfolio. Consistency keeps my "eye single" (3 Nephi 13:22) when school, work, and family compete for my attention.

Reflection

This week I found a quiet place at home early in the morning for my scripture study. I put my phone away, watched "Jesus Christ: The Prince of Peace," and pondered my covenants. I thought about how I got here. I once planned to study medicine, but when that door closed, I taught myself technology through YouTube. At the time, it felt like a detour. In that quiet moment I felt peace that the Lord had been guiding me all along, and that the skills I've gained are meant to serve others.

Elder Bednar's image of intertwined strands helped me see my life more clearly. My faith, my studies, and my work are not separate ropes pulling in different directions. When they are woven together in Christ, they become strong. I realized that my best professional moments, like building tools for schools or teaching a struggling student, were also my most spiritual ones. I also noticed that when I am too busy to pray, my work feels heavy and scattered.

Professionally, this reflection changed how I see my AI research. I study whether AI systems follow the truth they are given, and my quiet study at home reminded me that my own life must follow truth too. 3 Nephi 13:22 teaches that a single eye fills the whole body with light. For me, that means keeping Christ at the center so my career is a way of serving Him, not just a way of succeeding. Going forward, I will begin each workday with prayer and plan my projects around the people they serve.

Agency, Adversity, and Choosing to Act

New materials

  1. Doctrine and Covenants 58:26–28: "He that is compelled in all things, the same is a slothful and not a wise servant… men should be anxiously engaged in a good cause, and do many things of their own free will… For the power is in them, wherein they are agents unto themselves." (Principles 1 & 3)
  2. Joseph B. Wirthlin, "Come What May, and Love It," October 2008 general conference: "The way we react to adversity can be a major factor in how happy and successful we can be in life." (Principle 3)
  3. Dieter F. Uchtdorf, "Be Not Afraid, Only Believe," October 2015 general conference: "There is no magic to belief. But wanting to believe is the necessary first step!" (Principle 1)
  4. BYU–Idaho, "Problem Solving and Collaboration," in Organizational Leadership: A Christ-Centered Approach (2024): the five stages of group problem solving (define, generate, evaluate, pick, plan) and the counsel to keep a team "on the same stage and progressing together." (Principle 2)
  5. Personal experience: leading my TRI AI Saturdays team, testing software as an HNG Internship QA intern, and refining my IT 312 Metasploit report for Project 4. (Principles 2 & 3)

Reflection: The power is in me

This week I found a quiet place at home to ponder Doctrine and Covenants 58:26–28. Verse 28 stood out to me: "the power is in them, wherein they are agents unto themselves." I read it slowly several times and asked myself where I have waited to be commanded, and where I have chosen to act.

My answer took me back to the moment my plan to study medicine ended. I could have waited for another plan to arrive. Instead, I opened YouTube and started teaching myself technology, one lesson at a time. I did not see it as agency then, but I see it now. That small choice led to CloudCity, to more than 300 learners, to Bravick, and to my published benchmark. Elder Wirthlin's mother told him, "Come what may, and love it." He explained that she was not asking him to pretend pain away, but that "if we approach adversities wisely, our hardest times can be the times of greatest growth." A closed door became the start of my career.

President Uchtdorf's talk spoke to me as someone who evaluates AI for a living. He said belief "requires a little scientific curiosity—it requires an experiment upon the word of God." I spend my days designing careful experiments to test whether models follow the evidence they are given. His words reminded me that faith is not the opposite of careful thinking. It is a choice to test the word and act on what I learn. He also said, "Skepticism is easy—anyone can do it." In my research, doubt is a useful tool, but it cannot replace the courage to commit and build.

The BYU–Idaho leadership reading gave me words for something I learned as a team lead. Groups struggle when people are at different stages, so a leader keeps everyone "on the same stage and progressing together." In TRI AI Saturdays, my job was not to have every answer, but to help each person move forward with the team. I now see that as agency too: choosing to lift others instead of racing ahead.

Reflection itself has become one of my growth tools. When I stop, write, and ponder, I notice patterns I miss when I am busy. Going forward, I will end each week by asking three questions: What did I choose to do? What did I wait to be told? Who did my work bless?
