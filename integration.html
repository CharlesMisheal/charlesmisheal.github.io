Integration Reflection

A mind map of how my faith in Jesus Christ shapes my work in technology, the warning signs that tell me the two are drifting apart, and how I plan to stay aligned over time.

On this page

  • Mind map: Christ at the center
  • The reflective activity
  • What I realized
  • Personal warning signs and how I will realign
  • How I will maintain this alignment over time
  • Closing
  • References

Elder David A. Bednar compared the gospel to a rope: "Just as a rope obtains its strength from many intertwined individual strands," the gospel gives us its richest blessings as we "gather together in one all things in Christ" (Bednar, 2018). My earlier pages described those strands one at a time. This page shows how they connect, and what I will do when one of them starts to fray.

Mind map: Christ at the center

The reflective activity

This week I studied three resources at home: the Ensign College devotional by President and Sister Kusch, Sister Neill F. Marriott's devotional "Stand in Truth," and the BYU–Idaho leadership reading "Embracing Differences and Overcoming Contention." I read them beside my own portfolio, one page at a time, and asked my three weekly questions of each part of my work. Then I drew the map above. I started with Christ in the center because, as Elder Bednar taught, the power of the gospel "flows from discerning and applying the interrelatedness of its doctrine, principles, and practices" (Bednar, 2018). I wanted to see the connections, not just the parts.

What I realized

My faith is a rope, not a checklist

Elder Bednar warned that "pharisaical focus upon checklists can divert us from drawing closer to the Lord" (Bednar, 2018). When I looked at my portfolio honestly, I saw that I sometimes treat faith like a section to complete. Each project ends with a "Faith in Practice" note, and that is good, but faith should also shape the context, the decisions, and the ethics of each project, not just the last paragraph. Bravick is a good example of integration done well. The question "Who is this meant to bless?" changed the whole design, from offline-first exams to records kept on the school's own computer. That is the kind of integration I want in all of my work.

Understanding is something I do

President Kusch taught that "someone with understanding not only says what they know, but they act upon what they know. They go, and they do" (Kusch & Kusch, 2024). This hit close to home. In my AI research, I test whether language models follow the evidence they are given. A model can "know" the right answer from its context and still not act on it. I can do the same thing. I can write well about honesty, stewardship, and ministering, and still cut a corner when I am tired. For me, understanding is proven in small choices, like redacting a password hash in a practice lab or reporting that a model passed only 9 of 105 cases with a plain prompt.

Sister Kusch shared a promise that "whatever you have is an offering of faith," whether five minutes or an hour of scripture study, and she taught that "there is great power in recording these things" (Kusch & Kusch, 2024). That gave me a better way to think about busy weeks. Five honest minutes with the scriptures is better than waiting for a perfect hour that never comes. This portfolio is also part of that recording.

I do not pull the load alone

Sister Marriott quoted Lloyd Newell: "our confidence in the truth varies according to our connection to God. That is why daily habits are so important" (Marriott, 2023). She described being yoked to the Savior: "You, yoked to Jesus Christ, can pull an infinite amount of weight" (Marriott, 2023). As a returned missionary, I know that the Lord's work moves forward through daily, ordinary obedience. In my career, the load is different: classes, CloudCity learners, research, and deadlines. The lesson is the same. When I try to carry it alone, I feel heavy and scattered. When I begin with scripture study and prayer, I see more clearly what matters most. Sister Marriott also said, "Opening the scriptures is an act of faith" (Marriott, 2023). I want my morning study to come before I open my laptop, because that order is a statement of what comes first.

Christlike values speak to people of every belief

The BYU–Idaho reading asks, "How can these values be taught, modeled, and incorporated into a culture in which not all people believe in Jesus or in scriptures?" (BYU–Idaho, 2024). In technology, I will often work with people whose beliefs differ from mine. I do not need to win a religious argument to bring Christ into my work. I can model honesty, patience, and fairness, and explain my reasons in plain words that anyone can accept. The reading also notes that "with people from different backgrounds and understanding, contention will undoubtedly arise" (BYU–Idaho, 2024). The Savior's standard is higher than avoiding conflict. He said, "Be one; and if ye are not one ye are not mine" (Doctrine and Covenants 38:27). As a team lead, unity means listening first and making sure everyone feels they belong.

Patience is part of the process

The Savior taught, "In your patience possess ye your souls" (Luke 21:19). In technology it is easy to want fast results: a finished portfolio, a passing score, a working build. Bravick taught me that reliable systems are built through many small, careful choices, and discipleship works the same way. Patience with myself, with my learners, and with the Lord's timing is not a delay of growth. It is how growth happens.

Personal warning signs and how I will realign

Part of this activity was to become aware of the signs that my spiritual beliefs and professional practices are starting to drift apart. Naming them now helps me notice them early. Each sign is paired with a strategy for realignment.

How I will maintain this alignment over time

Alignment is not something I finish. It is something I keep, the way a system needs monitoring after it goes live. My plan has four rhythms:

  • Daily: scripture study at home and prayer before I open my laptop. Like Sister Marriott, I will keep a pen or a note open to record impressions as I read.
  • Weekly: on the Sabbath, I will answer my three questions: What did I choose to do? What did I wait to be told? Who did my work bless? I will compare my week against the warning signs in Table 1.
  • Each block: I will review this mind map, update at least one portfolio page, and choose one warning sign to work on. When I am able to attend the temple, I will take these questions with me and ponder them there.
  • After graduation in August 2027: as I move into AI evaluation and research, I will keep this map as a personal standard. New roles will bring new pressures, and I expect to add new warning signs as I learn more about myself.

I will also ask someone I trust to check in with me about these goals, because accountability makes small habits easier to keep.

Closing

When I began this portfolio, I described my faith and my work as strands that strengthen each other. This week helped me see that the strands only hold when they are woven around one center. Jesus Christ is that center. My principles come from Him, my professional practices are how I try to live them, and my warning signs show me when I have pulled away. I know I will drift at times. When I do, I can come back to Him, one small and simple step at a time, and with patience.
