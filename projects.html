Bravick

Offline-first school management and computer-based testing platform · TypeScript, React + Vite, Node/Express, JWT, SQLite, Electron

Context

Many schools want computer-based exams and digital records, but their internet is unreliable. Most tools assume a steady cloud connection, so an outage on exam day can stop everything. I wanted to build one system that runs a school's administration and its exams without depending on the internet.

Challenges

  • Exams had to keep running with no internet connection at all.
  • Many students needed to sit exams at the same time on phones, tablets, and laptops, without installing an app.
  • Results could not be lost if a laptop failed or the software was updated.
  • Teachers and parents needed records they could use, even where parents had no email or internet.

How I overcame them

I designed Bravick to run on one school computer as a desktop app built with Electron. A Node/Express server and a local SQLite database run on that computer, and staff and students connect over the school Wi‑Fi from any browser. JWT sign-in protects each account. The server keeps running in the background, so closing the window does not stop an exam in progress. Records are stored separately from the program, so updates do not delete results, and schools can back up to USB after important exams. I added automatic marking for multiple-choice and many short answers, a live proctor view, class broadsheets, and printable parent slips.

Results

Bravick is built and available to schools through CloudCity. It brings exams, attendance, fees, timetables, and lesson plans into one system that works on a campus network with no cloud connection required on exam day.

Lessons learned

I learned to design for the hardest day, not the easiest one. Offline-first is not a feature you add at the end. It shapes the database, the network setup, and how backups work from the start. I also learned that good software comes from listening closely to the people who will use it.

Faith in Practice: Bravick began with the question I try to ask of every project: "Who is this meant to bless?" Christ left the ninety and nine to find the one (Luke 15:4–7). Building for schools that most software overlooks is how I try to follow that pattern in my work. The project also taught me patience. Reliable systems, like discipleship, are built through many small and careful choices (Alma 37:6).

Evidence-Following in RAG

Published benchmark, 2026 · Python, LLM evaluation · doi:10.5281/zenodo.22151237 · Code on GitHub

Context

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) systems are usually judged by whether they find the right documents. But finding evidence is only half the job. The model also has to follow it. I wanted to measure whether language models stay with the evidence they are given, or add confident claims the evidence does not support.

Challenges

  • In a normal RAG system, retrieval errors and generation errors are mixed together, so it is hard to tell which part failed.
  • A fair test needed cases where the right answer is to say "the evidence doesn't say," or to point out that two sources disagree.
  • I had to report results honestly, including the ones that made models look weak.

How I overcame them

I placed the evidence directly in the prompt, which removed retrieval from the test so I could measure only whether the model followed the evidence. I grew a 15-case pilot into a benchmark of 105 agricultural questions across seven conditions: sufficient evidence, insufficient evidence, conflicting prior, conflicting evidence, irrelevant context, partial evidence, and attribution. I then tested each model with two prompts, a plain "be helpful" prompt and a careful evidence-constrained prompt, and reported results for each condition along with the study's limitations.

Results

I tested Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite, gpt-4.1-mini, and gpt-oss-20b. With the careful prompt, the models passed 96 to 103 of the 105 cases. With the plain prompt, they passed only 9 to 57. I published the benchmark on Zenodo in 2026 and shared the code on GitHub.

Lessons learned

The same model can be trustworthy or careless depending on how it is asked. Small wording choices have large effects, so evaluation has to test conditions one at a time. I also learned that a clear, honest negative result is more useful than a flattering one.

Faith in Practice: This project is about honesty. A student or farmer who asks an AI system for help deserves an answer that stays true to the evidence. Working on it strengthened my own commitment to being honest in how I report my work, even when the numbers are not impressive. I believe truth matters in small things, and I want to help make AI tools that people can trust.

Agricultural Document Retrieval Pipeline

Team lead, TRI AI Saturdays Cohort 10 · Python, TF-IDF, BM25, MiniLM embeddings, reranking · github.com/CharlesMisheal/C10-team-chobe

Context

During TRI AI Saturdays Cohort 10, where I also took Google DeepMind's AI Research Foundations courses, I served as team lead. Our team built a retrieval system for agricultural extension documents, so a farmer's question returns the most useful evidence first.

Challenges

  • Keyword search alone often missed the documents that mattered most.
  • We needed to prove that an improvement was real and not luck on one test split.
  • As team lead, I had to keep a group of learners moving forward together while we were all still learning the methods.

How we overcame them

We started with simple baselines (TF-IDF and BM25) so we had an honest point of comparison. We then added MiniLM semantic retrieval, which matches meaning rather than exact words, and a feature-based reranker. We checked the reranker with 5-fold validation and measured retrieval quality separately from answer generation. As team lead, I supported teammates through the curriculum and helped keep our work focused on the goal.

Results

Our pipeline raised nDCG@5 from 0.49 with the TF-IDF baseline to 0.74, meaning the most relevant documents appeared near the top far more often.

Lessons learned

Start with a simple baseline and measure every step. I also learned that leading a team is less about having every answer and more about helping each person contribute their best.

Faith in Practice: Leading this team felt like ministering. King Benjamin taught that "when ye are in the service of your fellow beings ye are only in the service of your God" (Mosiah 2:17). Helping a teammate understand a difficult idea mattered as much to me as improving the score. The work itself serves people who feed their families and communities, and better retrieval can help them find reliable guidance when they need it.
